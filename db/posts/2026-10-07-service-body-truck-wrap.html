Plumbers, electricians, HVAC techs, utility crews and municipal fleets run trucks that are not quite pickups: a cab in front and a service body behind it, built from a dozen compartment doors, drip rails, latches and hinges. A service body truck wrap has to deal with all of that, and the rules that work on a pickup bed or a van side do not transfer directly. This guide explains what makes utility bodies different, which surfaces take film and which do not, how to design around the doors, and when cut lettering beats a wrap altogether.

What a Service Body Is, from a Wrap Installer's Point of View

A service body (also called a utility body) replaces the pickup bed with a set of lockable compartments along both sides and an open or covered cargo area in the middle. The body is built by a specialist manufacturer, bolted to the chassis, and usually painted to match the cab or left in bare aluminum. From a wrap perspective it has five characteristics that a pickup does not:

  • Many small panels. Each compartment door is its own panel with four edges. A single side can have six or more doors, which means far more edge per square metre of film than a pickup bed.
  • Daily movement. Doors open and close dozens of times a day. Every door edge is handled, slammed and scraped by tools.
  • Hardware everywhere. Latches, handles, hinges, locks, lights, drip rails and reflectors interrupt every surface.
  • Mixed materials. Painted steel, painted aluminum, bare aluminum and diamond plate can all appear on one body.
  • Gear on top. Ladder racks, pipe carriers, cranes and compressors sit above the body and are bolted through it.

Which Surfaces Take a Service Body Truck Wrap

The manufacturers' substrate bulletins sort service body materials cleanly.

Painted steel and painted aluminum

Factory-painted body panels in good condition are standard wrap surfaces, prepared like any painted panel. The caution is rust: steel bodies rust first at hinges, door bottoms and where tools have chipped the paint. 3M is explicit that film must never go directly on carbon steel; corrosion has to be removed and the panel primed or painted first. Our rust repair guide covers the sequence.

Bare aluminum

Many utility bodies are unpainted aluminum. Both 3M and Avery Dennison prefer etched or anodised aluminum, and both say bare aluminum that has oxidised must have the oxidation removed and be degreased before film is applied. Smooth bare aluminum can be wrapped once prepared; weathered, chalky aluminum needs work first, and a shop should test adhesion before committing to a whole body.

Diamond plate and textured surfaces

Diamond plate (tread plate) cannot be wrapped. Its raised pattern is far rougher than the 150-grit smoothness 3M sets as the practical limit for adhesive contact, and film laid over it will bridge the pattern and lift. The same applies to spray-on bed liner on body tops and to textured plastic fender flares. Design around these areas or use lettering on the flat panels only.

Rubber seals, gaskets and sealant

Every compartment door has a rubber seal and most body joints carry sealant. 3M lists rubber and caulking materials as surfaces film does not reliably adhere to, so film stops at the painted edge of each door.

Designing Around the Doors

The compartment doors decide the layout. Three principles make a service body truck wrap read well:

  1. Treat each door as a tile. A graphic that flows across several doors is cut by every gap, latch and hinge line. Backgrounds and colour fields can flow; text and logos should sit whole on a single door or on the cab.
  2. Put the words where there is no hardware. The cab doors are the largest uninterrupted panels on the truck and the natural home for the company name and phone number. Large upper compartment doors come next. Keep lettering off latches, lock cylinders and reflectors.
  3. Use the rear. The rear compartment doors and the tailgate or bumper area are what drivers behind the truck see. A phone number and web address belong there, sized to be read from a car length back.

For the trades that run these trucks, the message hierarchy is the same one that works on vans: trade first, name second, contact third. Our articles on plumbing and HVAC van wraps and fleet decals for contractors go into what converts.

Wrap or Lettering?

On a service body truck wrap project, cut vinyl lettering and decals are often the better answer, not the budget compromise. Lettering has no large sheets to lift at door edges, each piece sits on one panel, a damaged letter is replaced on its own, and the many colours of a utility body (white cab, grey compartments, aluminum trim) are left to do their job. A printed wrap earns its place on the cab, where there are large smooth panels, and on a painted body with a few big doors. A common and durable layout is a partial truck wrap on the cab with truck lettering and decals on the body.

If the body is bare aluminum, the decision is simpler still: lettering and logo decals on the prepared aluminum, with the cab wrapped or lettered to match.

Racks, Cranes and the Gear on Top

A service body truck wrap also has to account for what sits on top. Ladder racks and pipe carriers bolt through the body top, and their feet sit on the surface the wrap would cover. Where the design runs across the top rails, the rack comes off before wrapping and goes back on afterwards; where it does not, the film ends at the rack feet. Cranes, compressors and inverters bring heat, hydraulic fluid and fuel into the picture, and film near them should be kept clear of exhaust outlets and fluid drips. Decide all of this before the truck arrives; our guide to preparing a truck for a wrap covers the accessories conversation.

Regulatory Lettering

Service trucks often operate under commercial rules, and Ontario's Highway Traffic Act requires commercial motor vehicles above certain weights to show the owner's name on both sides. Unit numbers, CVOR details and safety markings may also apply depending on the fleet. Design them in at the start, on a flat door, in a durable cut vinyl, rather than adding stickers afterwards. Our fleet decal compliance guide covers the practical side; confirm your own obligations with the Ministry of Transportation.

Durability Expectations on a Working Body

Be realistic about how a service body truck wrap wears. Film on compartment doors is handled, scraped by tools and pressure-washed at the edges in a way that film on a car door never is. Edges near latches and door bottoms will show wear first, and the lower band collects salt and gravel. That is an argument for designing so the high-wear pieces are small and replaceable: a lettering set or a single door panel can be redone in an hour, where a full side wrap cannot.
