Most camper conversions start life as a white cargo van. The interior becomes a home; the outside often stays a delivery van until the owner decides it should not. A camper van wrap is how a Sprinter, Transit or ProMaster conversion gets a colour of its own, a two-tone scheme or a set of graphics, without paint and without touching the resale value of the base vehicle. Conversions also bring things a work van never has: solar panels, roof fans, awnings, added windows, ladders and a fibreglass high-top on older vans. This guide covers what a camper wrap can do and how those additions change the job.

Why Camper Owners Wrap Instead of Paint

  • The base is usually a used commercial van. A respray on a vehicle this size is expensive and permanent. A wrap is reversible, which matters if you later sell the van to someone who wants it white again.
  • Colour and finish choices. Satin and matte earth tones, deep greens, sand, grey and two-tone schemes with a contrasting roof or lower band are all available as cast colour-change films. Our guide to gloss, satin and matte finishes explains how each one looks and ages.
  • Protection for the paint underneath. A wrap shields the factory paint from sun, tree sap and the swirl marks of campsite washing, as covered in does a vinyl wrap protect paint. It does not stop stone chips, which matters on gravel roads; see below.
  • Graphics that mean something. A trail map, a mountain line, a name for the van or the logo of the business that runs it, applied as cut vinyl or printed panels over the colour.

What Makes a Camper Van Wrap Different From a Work Van

Roof-mounted equipment

Solar panels, roof fans, racks and storage boxes are bolted through the roof skin and sealed. The roof is the hardest panel on any wrap because it is horizontal, so most conversions either leave the roof white and wrap the sides, or wrap the roof before the gear goes on. If the equipment is already mounted, the film is trimmed around each fitting, which leaves many edges on the one surface that takes the most sun and snow. Our roof wrap guide explains the horizontal-panel problem in detail. Sealant around the fittings is not a wrap surface; 3M lists rubber and caulking materials as surfaces film will not hold on.

Added windows and flares

Converters cut windows into panel vans and add bonded glass or framed windows with rubber trim. The wrap runs up to the trim and is tucked or trimmed; bonded glass with a painted ceramic border gives the cleanest edge. Wheel-arch flares and rock guards added by the converter are often textured plastic, which film does not reliably hold on, and are left as they are.

Fibreglass high-tops

Older conversions, and some new ones, carry a raised fibreglass roof cap. Fibreglass can be wrapped, but 3M calls for an outgassing test first: a sample of film is applied and left, and bubbles mean the resin is still releasing gas and the cap is not ready. Chalked or cracked gelcoat has to be dealt with before film goes on, because 3M warns that gelcoat that cracks will crack the film with it.

Awnings, ladders and swing-out carriers

Side awnings bolt along the roof edge, rear ladders and tire or box carriers bolt through the rear doors. Each one either comes off before wrapping or defines where the film stops. Removing them gives a seamless result and is usually worth the extra time on a vehicle you plan to keep.

Repainted panels

Used vans often have at least one repainted panel from a previous life. Film over fresh or poorly cured paint can bubble, and Avery Dennison does not warrant its films over aftermarket paint. Tell the shop which panels were resprayed so they can test adhesion rather than discover the problem.

Colour Change, Two-Tone or Graphics

Three approaches cover most camper van wrap projects:

  • Full colour change. Every painted panel in one film. On a high-roof van this is a large job with long vertical panels and at least one seam on the sides; a tall van's sides are wider than a roll of film, so seams are placed low and run with the body lines.
  • Two-tone. A contrasting lower band below the body line, often in a darker or textured film, with the factory white above. It covers the area that takes gravel and mud, costs less than a full wrap, and can be rewrapped on its own.
  • Graphics over the factory colour. Cut vinyl mountain lines, stripes, a name and a logo on the white van, the lightest and cheapest route.

The body-shape rules in our van wrap design guide apply here as well: keep detail out of the Sprinter's body channels, use the Transit's crease as a divider, and treat the ProMaster's flat sides as the big canvas they are. Gravel Roads, Campsites and Keeping the Wrap Alive

A camper van wrap goes where work vans do not: forestry roads, beaches, salted winter highways on the way to a ski hill. A few habits keep a camper van wrap looking good:

  • Protect the lower panels. Gravel chips film as readily as paint. A darker lower band hides chips; paint protection film on the lower panels and behind the wheels stops them. Our PPF vs vinyl wrap comparison explains the difference.
  • Wash by hand, and soon. Tree sap, bird droppings and bug splatter etch film if they sit in the sun. Campsite hose water and a soft mitt beat a pressure washer at a truck stop, and pressure washers should stay well back from edges and seams.
  • Mind the snow. Brush, do not scrape; our guide to removing ice and snow from a wrapped vehicle has the method.
  • Check the edges each season. Around roof fittings, window trim and door edges, where a lifted corner caught early is a two-minute fix.

Timing the Wrap in a Conversion

The best moment for a camper van wrap is after the bodywork and window cutting are finished and cured, and before roof equipment, awnings and carriers are mounted. That gives the installer clean, complete panels and lets the hardware go on over the film. If the van is already built out, the wrap can still be done; it simply involves more removal and more trimming. Either way the van needs to be indoors at shop temperature, which in the GTA means a heated bay for much of the year; see our guide to wrap timing in Toronto.
