A van is a rolling billboard, but not every van is the same billboard. A Mercedes Sprinter, a Ford Transit and a Ram ProMaster parked side by side offer three different canvases: different heights, different body lines, different places where the metal is flat and where it folds. Good van wrap design starts with the body you actually own, not with a logo dropped onto a generic template. This guide walks through what each common van shape does to a design, where the readable space really is, and how to brief a designer so the artwork works on your van rather than on paper.

Why the Body Style Drives Van Wrap Design

Vinyl follows the metal. Every rib, crease, door gap and handle on a van either breaks up the artwork or has to be designed around. On a flat trailer side you can put anything anywhere; on a van, a phone number that runs across a deep body channel becomes hard to read, and a logo centred on the sliding door disappears every time the door is open on a job site.

The body also sets what film and technique the installer uses. 3M rates its cast wrap films for flat surfaces, rivets, simple and compound curves, corrugations and deep channels, but each of those shapes takes more time and more skill than a flat panel, and the minimum application temperature rises for rivets and corrugations. Avery Dennison recommends its MPI 1105 print film with a conformable overlaminate for rivets and corrugations for the same reason. Designing with the body in mind therefore saves money as well as making the van easier to read.

Mercedes Sprinter: Ribs, Channels and Height

Sprinter van wrap design starts with the body lines. The Sprinter is tall, long and, in most generations, has pronounced horizontal body lines pressed into the sides. On the current body these form deep channels that run most of the length of the van; older models have a mix of ribs and flat window-blank panels.

  • Keep text out of the channels. Lettering that crosses a deep rib bends with it and reads badly from the side. Place your business name and phone number in the flat band above or below the channel line, and let background colour or graphics run through the ribs instead.
  • Use the height. A high-roof Sprinter has a tall flat area above the belt line that pedestrians and drivers in cars see at eye level. That is prime space for the one message you want remembered.
  • Plan for the rear doors. Sprinter rear doors are tall and split down the middle with hinges on the outside edges. Anything that spans both doors is cut by the gap, so keep the phone number and web address on one door or above and below the split.

The partial wrap we did for VMB Group's Mercedes Sprinter shows how colour blocks can run across the body lines while the lettering stays on the flat band.

Ford Transit: The Crease and the Sliding Door

Van wrap design on a Transit is easier in one way and trickier in another. Its sides are smoother than a Sprinter's, which makes it a friendlier canvas, but it has two features that catch designers out.

  • The belt-line crease. A strong horizontal crease runs through the doors at roughly handle height. It is not deep, but it is sharp enough that a straight line of text crossing it will look bent from some angles. Use it as a natural divider: one message above, one below.
  • The sliding door track. The recessed track for the sliding door runs along the lower rear quarter on the passenger side. It is a channel the film has to drop into, so avoid fine detail there.
  • Roof heights change the proportions. A low-roof Transit has a wide, short canvas; a high-roof version has a tall one. The same artwork will not fit both without rework, which matters if your fleet mixes them.

Our electrician client's Ford Transit partial wrap and Alpha Katering's three-van fleet are both Transits designed around that crease.

Ram ProMaster: Big Flat Sides and a Boxy Shape

For van wrap design the ProMaster is the most billboard-like of the three. Its sides are tall and comparatively flat, its roof line is boxy, and its front end is short. That gives you the largest uninterrupted panels of any common van, which suits full-colour printed wraps and large photographic graphics.

  • Use the flat sides for impact. Large imagery, bold colour fields and oversized lettering all work here without fighting body lines.
  • Watch the wheel arches. They are prominent and sit high, and a design that ignores them will have a logo sliced by the arch.
  • The rear doors open wide. ProMaster rear doors can swing to 260 degrees. Graphics on the inside edges and on the door jambs will be seen when the van is being loaded, which can be a bonus or a distraction depending on the design.

Our Ram ProMaster partial wrap for Zuccarini is an example of using those flat sides for a bold, simple layout.

Compact Vans: Transit Connect, ProMaster City and Minivans

Small vans have more curves per square metre than their larger siblings. The Transit Connect and ProMaster City have rounded roof lines, sculpted doors and smaller flat areas, and converted minivans add sliding doors on both sides. On these vehicles:

  • Keep the design simpler. One strong colour, one logo, one phone number reads better than a busy layout squeezed into small panels.
  • Think about the rear. Compact vans are often seen from behind in traffic more than from the side. The tailgate or rear doors deserve the phone number and web address.
  • Expect more installation time per square metre than on a big van, because almost every panel is a compound curve.

Windows, Doors and the Things That Move

Whatever the van, three features need a decision before the van wrap design is finalised:

  • Windows versus panels. A cargo van with solid sides gives you the whole side; a crew van with windows gives you a lower band only, unless you use perforated window film, which we cover in this article.
  • Sliding doors. Any graphic on the sliding door is hidden when the door is open, and a graphic that spans the door gap is split when it is closed and misaligned when it is partly open. Put the critical text on the fixed panels.
  • Door handles, fuel doors and locks. Small, but a phone number with a handle through the middle of it is a common mistake. Our article on common van wrap mistakes lists the rest.

Reading Distance: Design for the Driver Behind You

Most people see a work van from another vehicle, from behind or alongside, at speed, for a few seconds. That changes the hierarchy. A van side can carry a large image and a slogan, but the information that converts, your trade, your area and how to reach you, has to be readable from the next lane. Our guides on how van lettering converts traffic into customers and on QR codes on van wraps go deeper on what to put where.

Full, Partial or Lettering: Matching Coverage to the Body

Body style also tips the van wrap design choice between a full van wrap, a partial van wrap and van lettering and decals. A white ProMaster with huge flat sides can look complete with a partial wrap that uses white as part of the design. A Sprinter with deep body lines often looks better fully wrapped in a colour, with lettering on the flat band, than with a partial that stops at a rib. How to Brief a Van Wrap Design

  1. Tell the designer the exact van: make, model, year, roof height, wheelbase, and whether it has windows or a sliding door on each side.
  2. Send straight-on photos of each side and the rear, in daylight, with the doors closed. Our guide on photographing your vehicle for a wrap design explains the angles.
  3. List the must-haves in priority order: name, trade, phone, web address, service area, licence numbers. The designer places the top three where they read best for your body style.
  4. Supply vector artwork for the logo, as described in our file types guide.
  5. Ask for a proof on your van, not a template. A proof on the right body with the right roof height is the only way to see what the ribs and doors do to the layout.
