A box truck is the biggest moving sign most businesses will ever own. Two sides of ten to twenty-six feet each, a rear door seen by everyone stuck behind it, and a cab that carries the brand into the parking lot. It is also one of the harder vehicles to wrap well, because the box is built from materials and shapes a car never has: riveted aluminum, corrugated panels, fibreglass-reinforced plastic, roll-up doors and seams every few feet. This box truck wrap guide explains what makes the box different, how the right film and preparation handle rivets and corrugations, how to design for a panel that size, and what Ontario expects to see on the side of a commercial truck.

What Makes a Box Truck Wrap Different

On a car, the installer's challenge is curves. On a box truck it is surface. The cargo box is a separate structure bolted behind the cab, and it is built one of three ways:

  • Riveted aluminum sheet. Thin aluminum panels fastened to posts with rows of rivets every few inches. Common on older and heavier-duty boxes.
  • Corrugated or ribbed panels. Metal sides pressed with vertical or horizontal ribs for stiffness. Found on many cube vans and refrigerated bodies.
  • FRP (fibreglass-reinforced plastic) panels. Smooth, seamless-looking composite sides with a gelcoat finish, often with aluminum extrusions at the corners. Common on newer dry-freight boxes.

Each one is a different wrap job. Smooth FRP is the easiest to wrap and the most forgiving to design for. Rivets and corrugations are wrappable, but they need specific films, more heat, more time and higher shop temperatures, and they change how fine detail reads from a distance.

Film Choice for Rivets and Corrugations

Film choice decides whether a box truck wrap survives its first winter. Both major manufacturers address rivets directly. Avery Dennison's data sheet for its MPI 1105 cast print film says that combined with its DOL 1460Z or DOL 1480Z conformable overlaminates the film is highly recommended for rivets and corrugations, and lists large fleet graphics on flat, curved, riveted and corrugated surfaces as a primary use. 3M rates its IJ180 cast print films for rivets and corrugations as well, with two cautions in the bulletin: expect some tenting around rivets, and the minimum application temperature is higher for rivets and corrugations than for flat panels.

The practical implications:

  • Cast film only. A calendered film will not stay down around a rivet head. Our article on cast vs calendered vinyl explains why.
  • A conformable laminate. The laminate over a printed box wrap has to be as conformable as the film, or the sheet will lift off the rivets as a unit. See our guide to vehicle wrap laminates.
  • Heat and time. Every rivet is worked individually with heat and a rivet brush. A side with hundreds of rivets takes far longer than a smooth FRP side of the same size, and the quote will reflect it.
  • A heated bay. 3M's application temperatures for IJ180C start at 4°C for flat panels but 10°C for rivets and corrugations, and Avery Dennison specifies 10°C minimum for MPI 1105. In practice shops work well above those minimums, which in the GTA means indoors from autumn to spring. Our guide to wrap timing in Toronto covers the seasons.

Preparing the Box: FRP, Aluminum and Steel

3M's substrate preparation bulletin treats each box material separately, and the differences matter.

FRP panels

Fibreglass can outgas, releasing gases from the resin that form bubbles under a freshly applied film. 3M calls for an outgassing test on FRP before wrapping: a sample of film is applied and left, and if bubbles appear the panel is not ready. The bulletin also warns that gelcoat that cracks will crack the film with it, and that chalked gelcoat has to be buffed off before cleaning. Avery Dennison's substrate bulletin gives a similar test for new and weathered fibreglass.

Aluminum

Painted or anodised aluminum is cleaned like paint. Bare, oxidised aluminum has to have the oxidation removed and be degreased first; both manufacturers prefer etched or anodised surfaces. 3M adds a specific note for riveted metal: make sure no moisture remains under the rivets or in body seams before film goes on.

Steel and rust

Box corners, door frames and the lower edges of the box are where rust appears, and 3M is categorical: film must not be applied directly to carbon steel. Corrosion has to be removed and the surface primed or painted, then prepared as a painted surface. Our article on rust repair before a wrap covers the repair sequence and cure times.

For the general preparation steps that apply to the cab and box alike, from washing without wax to removing old graphics, read how to prepare your truck for a wrap.

Roll-Up Doors, Swing Doors and Seams

The rear of a box truck wrap is the panel most people see, from directly behind in traffic, and it is the hardest to wrap cleanly.

  • Roll-up doors are made of horizontal slats, each hinged to the next. A graphic can be printed across the whole door and applied slat by slat, with a small gap at every hinge line, so fine text across the slats breaks up. Large, bold shapes and a phone number sized to one or two slats read well; small print does not.
  • Swing (barn) doors are flat and split down the middle. Design so nothing critical crosses the gap.
  • Panel seams on riveted and corrugated boxes run vertically every few feet. Film is usually seamed at the same places, with overlaps that face away from the direction of travel so wind and washing do not catch the edge.

Designing for a Panel This Big

A box side is a billboard, and billboard rules apply. The people who see it are in cars, at speed, 20 to 60 metres away. That argues for:

  • One message per side. Business name, what you do, how to reach you. Everything else is decoration.
  • Letter heights measured in tens of centimetres. Text that looks huge on a proof is often too small on a 16-foot box. Ask to see the proof at scale against the door height.
  • Contrast over colour count. Dark text on a light field or the reverse survives dirt, dusk and rain better than a subtle palette.
  • The rear door as the conversion panel. Drivers stuck behind you have time to read a phone number or scan a QR code. Put it there, sized to the slats.
  • Imagery that tolerates ribs and rivets. Photographic faces and fine line art distort over corrugations. Bold shapes, textures and colour fields do not.

Full Box Wrap, Partial Wrap or Lettering

Because the box is so large, a full box truck wrap uses a lot of material and labour. The usual options are:

  • Full wrap of the box and cab for a colour change plus branding, the most impactful and the most expensive to install and later remove.
  • Box-only wrap, leaving the cab in its factory colour. Very common, because the cab is where the curves and the cost are.
  • Partial wrap that uses the box's white or factory colour as part of the design, with printed panels placed where they read best.
  • Lettering and decals cut from coloured cast vinyl, the lowest-cost option and a strong one on a plain white box.

Ontario Markings on a Commercial Truck

Before the design is finalised, settle the regulatory lettering. Ontario's Highway Traffic Act requires a commercial motor vehicle to carry a sign showing the name of the owner on both sides, in a clearly visible position. The requirement has exemptions, and one of them, as summarised by compliance publishers, is a registered gross weight of 18,000 pounds or less unless the vehicle is licensed as a public vehicle or public truck. Many box trucks sit above that threshold, and operators under the Commercial Vehicle Operator's Registration (CVOR) programme may have further obligations.

Confirm what applies to your truck with the Ministry of Transportation, then design the required lettering into the wrap rather than adding stickers after the fact. Our fleet decal compliance guide covers the practical side.

Installation, Downtime and Aftercare

A box side is installed from a lift or scaffold, often by two installers working a panel together, and the box has to be indoors at shop temperature. Expect a box truck wrap to take longer than a van of the same length, and longer again for rivets. For fleets, stage the trucks so the business keeps running; our article on fleet wrap installation explains how.

Afterwards, the main enemies are pressure washers and brush washes at the seams and rivet heads. Hand washing, keeping the pressure wand well back from edges, and a yearly inspection of rivet lines and door slats keep a box wrap looking new.
