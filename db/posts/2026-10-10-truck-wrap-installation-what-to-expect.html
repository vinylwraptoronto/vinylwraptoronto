Handing a work truck to a wrap shop for several days raises practical questions. What happens to it while it is gone? Why does the shop need it longer than a sign would take? And what should you do differently once it comes back? This guide walks through a professional truck wrap installation stage by stage, from drop-off to pickup, so you know what to expect and what each step is protecting.

The stages below follow the written application procedures of 3M and Avery Dennison. Where the two give different figures, both are shown, because the film on your truck decides which applies.

Before the truck wrap installation: what the shop needs from you

A truck wrap installation starts before the truck arrives. The shop needs approved artwork, a confirmed scope (full, partial or lettering) and an honest picture of the truck's condition. Fresh paint, rust, body filler and old graphics all change the plan, and discovering them on installation day costs time for both sides.

If you have not already done it, work through our truck wrap preparation checklist. Empty the cab and the bed of anything the installers would have to move, and make sure the truck arrives washed. 3M's vehicle application guidance asks for a wash before the inspection for a simple reason: problem areas are easier to see on a clean panel.

Stage 1: Drop-off and the pre-installation inspection

Expect a walk-around with an installer, not just a key handover. Every truck wrap installation should begin this way. 3M recommends that installers complete and sign a pre-installation inspection record for each vehicle before any film goes on. The installer checks that the paint is sound, meaning well bonded, free of loose paint, rust, blistering, dents and filler, and circles anything that could affect adhesion or that removal could later damage.

Those areas are photographed and recorded, and you may be asked to sign the form. This is not the shop covering itself at your expense. A documented problem area is one that both sides know about, and it is the record a manufacturer warranty claim relies on later. Primer can be applied to poor paint spots, but 3M is clear that doing so does not guarantee the film will hold there.

If the inspection turns up rust, the usual answer is to postpone that panel; our guide to rust repair before a vehicle wrap explains why.

Stage 2: Cleaning and decontamination

A truck that looks clean is still contaminated as far as the film is concerned, and cleaning is the longest unseen part of a truck wrap installation. Road film, wax, tar, tree sap and polish all sit between the adhesive and the paint, and the adhesive bonds to whatever it touches first. 3M describes a sequence that moves from a mild detergent wash, to drying, to a solvent wipe, to a final pass with a 70/30 mix of isopropyl alcohol and water. For colour-change work it goes further and recommends a dedicated day for cleaning and inspection, followed by cleaning each panel again immediately before film is applied to it.

Work trucks need particular attention where grime collects: lower doors and rockers, behind the rear wheels, tailgate seams, the fuel door, and around rivets and bed rails. Avery Dennison's application bulletin singles out edges, corners, welding seams and rivets as critical areas that must be thoroughly cleaned and dried before application.

Wax deserves its own mention. 3M warns that car wax can greatly reduce graphic adhesion and must be removed with a solvent-based cleaner. If your truck has been through a wash that applies a spray wax or sealant, tell the shop.

Stage 3: Removing or masking hardware

Installers decide, panel by panel, whether to remove a part or wrap around it. On a truck wrap installation this decision shapes both the finish and the schedule. Door handles, mirror caps, badges, lights, mud flaps, bed-rail caps, tailgate handles and aftermarket toolboxes can all be in the way. Removing a part lets the film tuck behind it for a clean, sealed edge. Working around a part is faster but leaves a cut edge that must be sealed carefully.

On a work truck the decision often comes down to how the part is attached: badges peel off, bolted-on racks may be simpler to wrap around, and sensors on newer trucks may not be worth disturbing. Ask your installer what they intend to remove, and say if there is anything you do not want touched.

Stage 4: Applying the film panel by panel

This is the part most people picture, and it is the least mysterious. A full truck wrap installation is a series of panels, each applied dry (3M and Avery Dennison both specify dry application for their wrap films), squeegeed from the centre out so trapped air can escape through the adhesive's air-release channels, and trimmed at the panel edge.

3M's colour-change procedure describes a deliberate order: flat areas first, where the film lies naturally; then the complex compound areas that need pre-stretching, such as bumpers, mirrors and handles; then rounded compound shapes; and finally recesses and channels. Heat is used during this stage only to soften the film so it conforms. 3M gives 40 to 50°C for its 2080 film and Avery Dennison gives 40 to 55°C for its Supreme Wrapping Film, which is far below the post-heating temperature in the next stage.

One detail separates a durable job from one that lifts within the year: the film is cut at every body-panel seam. 3M's guidance is blunt: seams flex as the vehicle moves, and film that bridges them will pull away. For the particular challenges of box bodies, rivets and corrugations, see our box truck wrap guide.

Temperature matters throughout. 3M's vehicle graphics bulletin calls for the air and the vehicle surface to both be between 16 and 32°C, and Avery Dennison sets 10°C as the absolute minimum, with 21 to 27°C as the ideal. This is why a reputable shop wraps indoors, and why a truck that has sat outside in a Toronto January needs time to warm up before anyone unrolls film.

Stage 5: Post-heating, the step you cannot see afterwards

Post-heating is the quality step of a truck wrap installation that is invisible on the finished truck and missed most often on a rushed one. After the film is applied, every edge, overlap, recess and stretched area is reheated with a heat gun to a much higher temperature than during application, and the installer measures the film with an infrared thermometer as they go. This relieves the stress in stretched vinyl, closes any remaining air channels and lets the adhesive fully wet out onto the paint.

The target depends on the film. 3M specifies 80 to 95°C for its 2080 colour-change film and 93 to 107°C for its printed graphic films. Avery Dennison specifies 90 to 95°C for its Supreme Wrapping Film. 3M also notes that the clear protective layer on its gloss 2080 films must be removed before post-heating, and that applying heat to recesses is required, not optional. On a truck, that means the door recesses, the body lines on the bed sides, the area around the fuel door and the tailgate edges all get this treatment.

If you want one question that shows whether a shop takes certified installation methods seriously, ask how they post-heat and how they measure it.

Stage 6: Final inspection and pickup

Before the truck is released at the end of the truck wrap installation, the installer re-squeegees all edges and overlaps, reassembles any hardware that came off, cleans the glass and checks the whole body under good light. Do your own walk-around at pickup. Look along the panels at a low angle for bubbles, lifted edges and misaligned graphics, open and close the doors and the tailgate, and check that the artwork reads correctly from both sides and the rear.

Ask for three things before you leave: completion photos, the care instructions for the specific film used, and a note of the film and laminate products on the truck. That record makes a future colour-matched panel repair or a second vehicle much simpler.

Stage 7: Wrap aftercare in the first days after a truck wrap installation

The adhesive is still building its bond when you drive away, which is why the first few days after a truck wrap installation have their own rules. The manufacturers' written guidance is specific:

  • Keep it warm. 3M asks that the vehicle stay above 16°C for at least 12 hours after installation before it is exposed to a cold or wet climate, to strengthen the bond in the contoured areas. In a Toronto winter, that can mean leaving the truck at the shop overnight or parking it inside.
  • Let it set before it works. Avery Dennison asks for 24 hours for graphics to fully set before a vehicle goes back into service.
  • Do not wash it yet. 3M's figure is at least 24 hours for printed vehicle graphics and at least 72 hours for 2080 colour-change film. Avery Dennison's is 48 hours. If you are unsure which film is on your truck, use the longest.
  • Wash it gently afterwards. Hand washing is the preferred method for both manufacturers. If you use a pressure washer, keep it away from the film edges; 3M warns that high-pressure spray directed at edges causes lifting. Our guide to pressure washing a wrapped vehicle covers the distances and nozzles.
  • Wipe, do not polish. Avery Dennison suggests a microfibre wipe-down after installation to remove fingerprints and dust. Leave waxes and polishes alone unless the film's care guide approves them.

How long does a truck wrap take?

There is no honest universal answer, and a shop that quotes a truck wrap installation time without seeing the truck is guessing. The factors below are what actually set the schedule, and they are the questions a good estimator will ask you.

What changes the installation window for a truck wrap
FactorWhy it matters
CoverageA full truck wrap covers every painted panel, including the ones that need the most heat and trimming. A partial truck wrap or lettering and decals involve far less edge work.
Body typeA pickup is mostly flat panels and a bed. A service body, a cube van or a dump body adds compartment doors, rivets, corrugations and bare aluminum, each with its own preparation.
Paint and surface conditionRust, repairs, fresh paint that is still outgassing and old graphics that must come off first all add time, sometimes days.
HardwareEvery part that is removed and reinstalled, and every part that is wrapped around instead, is a decision with a time cost.
Design complexityA single colour is forgiving. A printed design with lines that must flow across doors and seams needs careful alignment panel by panel.
Conditions after installationThe post-installation warm period and the no-wash window are part of the schedule, even though the truck is back in your hands.

Ask the shop for a vehicle-specific window after they have seen the truck and the artwork, and plan downtime around that rather than a generic figure from a website. If you run several trucks, see our guide on planning a fleet wrap rollout.

Questions worth asking your installer

  • Which film and laminate will go on the truck, and can I have the manufacturer's care sheet for it?
  • What did the pre-installation inspection find, and is any area being treated as a known problem?
  • What hardware are you removing, and what are you wrapping around?
  • How do you post-heat, and how do you check the temperature?
  • Where will the truck sit overnight, and can it stay indoors for the warm period after installation?
  • When can I wash it, and when can it go back on the road?

A shop that answers these without hesitation is one that follows the manufacturer's truck wrap process rather than improvising a truck wrap installation.

Ready to book your truck?

A truck wrap installation done properly is a sequence of unglamorous steps you will never see on the finished vehicle: a signed inspection, a thorough decontamination, film cut at every seam, post-heating measured with a thermometer, and a few careful days before the first wash. Those steps are what the manufacturers' warranties assume, and they separate a wrap that lasts from one that lifts at the edges by spring.
