Most truck owners see two moments of a wrap: the day they hand over the keys and the day they get the truck back. Everything in between decides how the wrap looks in year three. This guide walks through a professional truck wrap installation from the quote to the first wash, so you know what the shop is doing, why it takes the time it takes, and what you can do before drop-off to help. The steps apply to a work pickup getting commercial graphics, a crew cab getting a colour change and a box truck getting printed fleet branding.

Why a Truck Wrap Installation Takes Longer Than You Expect

Wrap film is a pressure-sensitive adhesive product, and the manufacturers are clear that vehicle wrapping is the most demanding thing you can ask of it. Avery Dennison's application bulletin for its Supreme Wrapping Film says that shortcuts in the process drastically increase the chance of problems during or after the installation. 3M's colour-change application guide recommends a dedicated day just for cleaning and inspecting the vehicle before any film goes on.

Trucks add their own complications: tailgates, bed rails, fender flares and running boards on a pickup; rivets, corrugated panels and roll-up doors on a box truck. The wrap installation process is a sequence of small, careful jobs, and the order matters.

Step 1: The Survey, the Quote and the Design Proof

Before any film is ordered for a truck wrap installation, the installer needs to know what the truck is, how much of it is being covered, and what is going on it. For a pickup, the coverage decision is usually a full truck wrap, a partial truck wrap over the factory paint, or truck lettering and decals. For a box truck, the question is which faces of the box carry graphics and whether the cab is included; our box truck wrap page covers those options.

Expect to send photos of every side of the truck and confirm the exact model, cab and bed length, because panel sizes change between trims. If the wrap carries printed graphics, you will approve a design proof that fixes where the logo sits relative to door handles, wheel arches and body lines. Our guide to vehicle wrap file types explains what the printer needs from your artwork, and our fleet decal compliance guide covers the regulatory markings a commercial truck has to keep visible.

Agree the practical details in writing before you pay a deposit: the film brand and series, whether printed film is laminated, what will be removed and refitted, the timeline, and what happens if the inspection finds paint problems. Our vehicle wrap checklist lists the questions to ask.

Step 2: Preparing the Truck for Drop-Off

An hour of preparation shortens the truck wrap installation and reduces the chance of surprises.

  • Wash the truck, but do not wax it. 3M's guide says every vehicle should arrive washed, and Avery Dennison's bulletin says not to apply polishes, waxes or tyre-shine products after the wash. A clean truck lets both of you see the paint during the inspection.
  • Tell the installer about coatings. Avery Dennison's bulletin states that the surface must be free of wax, polish, copolymer and ceramic coatings. Removing a ceramic coating or dealer-applied sealant is extra work that is better quoted in advance.
  • Empty the bed and the cab, and take off ladder racks, toolboxes, hitch accessories and magnetic signs at your yard. Agree which items the shop will remove and refit.
  • Photograph existing damage: dents, chips, rust bubbles and old repairs. The installer will record the same, and the two should agree.

Step 3: The Inspection and the Deep Clean

This is the part of a truck wrap installation customers rarely see and the one that matters most. 3M recommends that installers complete a signed pre-installation inspection record for every vehicle. It identifies paint that is not sound: loose or poorly bonded paint, surface damage, body filler, rust and blistering, plus areas where water collects. Film applied over those areas can lift, and removing it later can take the paint with it, so the manufacturer recommends documenting them and agreeing in writing how they will be handled.

Rust is the most common finding on work trucks in the GTA, and wrapping over it does not stop it. If the inspection turns up bubbling at the wheel arches or rockers, read our guide to rust repair before a vehicle wrap. Fresh paint from a repair also has to cure for the period the paint manufacturer specifies before it can be wrapped; both 3M and Avery Dennison put that responsibility on the installer and the owner.

Once the paint is signed off, the cleaning starts. 3M's sequence for a colour change is a mild soap wash, compressed air to blow water and dust out of gaps and seams, a wipe-down with a 70 percent isopropyl alcohol and 30 percent water mix, solvent removal of tar, sap and old wax, a clay bar pass, and a final alcohol wipe. Avery Dennison adds a simple test: drag the back of a fingernail across the paint, and if it slides easily the surface still has residue on it. Each panel is cleaned again on installation day immediately before film touches it.

Step 4: Taking Hardware Off the Truck

A good installer removes what gets in the way of a clean edge rather than cutting around it. On a pickup that typically means door handles, mirror caps, badges, tail lights, the tailgate handle and camera trim, bed rail caps and mud flaps, and sometimes the bumper cover and fender flares. Running boards usually come off so the film can wrap under the rocker. On a box truck it means lights, reflectors, grab handles, latches and bolted-on signage.

Not everything should be wrapped. Avery Dennison's bulletin says film should not go over rubber and plastic mouldings, and chrome bumpers are a known problem that our bumper wrap guide explains. Agree before the job which parts are wrapped, left in factory finish, or painted first, and ask how the shop handles the clips that break on older trucks.

Step 5: Applying the Film

The application happens in a clean, enclosed, well-lit bay with the truck at a stable temperature. Avery Dennison recommends 16°C to 25°C for its Supreme Wrapping Film, and 3M says it is best practice to have both the air and the vehicle surface above 16°C and the surface below 32°C. Too cold and the adhesive bonds poorly and the film will not hold a stretch; too warm and the film grabs before it can be positioned. This is why a reputable shop will not do a truck wrap installation outdoors or in an unheated garage during a Toronto winter.

3M's guide sets the order of work: flat areas first, where the film lies naturally; then compound areas such as bumpers, mirrors and door handles that need pre-stretching; then the rounded shapes; and finally recesses and channels. The film is laid dry and squeegeed from the centre outward with overlapping strokes. A heat gun softens the film to around 40°C to 50°C so it can be conformed into curves, and the installer spreads the stretch over a wide area rather than one spot. Avery Dennison states that most wrapping needs no more than a 15 to 20 percent stretch and that 30 percent is the limit for its film.

At every panel join, the film is cut. Truck body panels flex independently when loaded or driven on rough ground, and film bridged across a seam will eventually tear. On a box truck wrap, the installer works film into corrugations and over rivets with a rivet brush and heat. 3M notes that some tenting around rivet heads is normal with printed fleet film rather than a defect, so ask how the rivets will look before the job rather than after.

Step 6: Post-Heating and the Final Check

Post-heating is the stage of a truck wrap installation that separates a wrap that stays put from one that pulls out of recesses in the first hot week. Stretched vinyl wants to return to its original shape; heating the stretched areas to a measured temperature resets that memory and locks the adhesive down. 3M specifies 80°C to 95°C for its 2080 films, measured with an infrared thermometer. Avery Dennison specifies 95°C to 100°C, no sooner than 30 to 45 minutes after application and on the same day the film went on.

Once the film cools, the installer re-squeegees every edge and overlap, which both manufacturers describe as essential to prevent edge lifting. Trapped air is released with a pin tool rather than a blade. Then the hardware goes back on and the truck is inspected panel by panel, ideally with you present. Any shop can say it post-heats; ask whether it measures the temperature. Our article on why certified installers matter explains what manufacturer certification does and does not guarantee.

Step 7: Pickup and the First 72 Hours

The adhesive keeps building its bond after you drive away, so the first days after a truck wrap installation have rules. The figures below come from 3M's colour-change guide for its 2080 films; Avery Dennison publishes its own maintenance bulletin with similar advice, and your installer will tell you which applies to the film on your truck.

  • Keep the truck above 16°C for at least 12 hours before exposing it to cold or wet weather. In spring and fall that can mean a night in the shop or your garage rather than on the street.
  • Do not wash the truck for at least 72 hours. After that, hand washing or a touchless wash with ordinary car soap is fine.
  • Keep pressure washers away from the edges. 3M warns that high-pressure spray aimed at film edges causes lifting.
  • Dry the film after washing to avoid hard-water spotting, which shows most on dark and matte finishes.
  • Ask before refitting magnetic signs or adhesive-mounted accessories. The installer will tell you when the wrap has cured enough.

Avery Dennison's bulletin instructs installers to hand over its maintenance guidance at completion, and a good shop will do the same. For a year-round routine, see our vehicle wrap maintenance tips, and before the first snowfall read our guide on clearing ice and snow from a wrapped vehicle.

What Is Different About a Commercial Truck Wrap Installation

A pickup truck wrap for a contractor and a colour change on a personal truck follow the same seven steps, but a commercial job has extra decisions.

  • Printed film versus coloured film. Commercial graphics are printed on a print wrap film and then laminated, and the laminate is what protects the ink from sun and washing, so confirm it is included. Our comparison of 3M, Avery Dennison and KPMF films covers the brands we work with.
  • Downtime. A work truck off the road is lost revenue, so agree the drop-off and pickup dates in writing. The Apex Property Management GMC Sierra and the ATM Systems box truck show how partial commercial wraps are planned around a working vehicle.
  • Coverage that fits the job. A partial wrap that leaves the lower body in factory paint installs faster, costs less and hides the gravel rash a work truck collects. Our article on truck wraps versus truck decals helps you decide how much coverage you need.
  • Future removal. If the truck is leased or the branding will change, ask how the film is rated for removal. See our guides on wrapping a leased vehicle in Ontario and removing truck decals without damaging paint.

Six Questions to Ask Before Booking a Truck Wrap Installation

  1. Will you inspect and document the paint before you start, and what happens if you find rust or filler?
  2. Which parts will you remove, which will you cut around, and which will stay in factory finish?
  3. What film series and laminate are you using, and is the bay heated to the manufacturer's application range?
  4. Do you post-heat with a measured temperature?
  5. What are the aftercare rules for the first week, and will you give them to me in writing?
  6. How long will the truck be off the road, and what is the plan if the inspection changes that?

A shop that answers all six without hesitation is following a truck wrap installation process. A shop that waves them off is the one to avoid, whatever the price.
