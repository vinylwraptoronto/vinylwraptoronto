The logo looked right on screen, right on the business cards, and slightly wrong on the van. Anyone who has branded a fleet has met this problem, and it is not carelessness: a printed wrap reproduces colour in a different way from a monitor, a business card or a paint chip, and some brand colours cannot be printed exactly at all. Good wrap colour matching is about knowing which colours will match, how to prove it before the van is wrapped, and when to switch from printed film to a solid colour vinyl. This guide covers all three.

Why Brand Colours Shift on a Printed Wrap

Screens show RGB; printers mix CMYK

The first wrap colour matching problem is the screen. A monitor makes colour from red, green and blue light. A wide-format printer makes it from cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink laid on white film. The range of colours each can produce (its gamut) is different, and the printer's is smaller. Vivid oranges, bright greens, teals, purples and anything neon sit outside what four inks can reproduce, so the printer produces the nearest colour it can. Many brand colours were chosen on a screen and have never been tested against ink.

Pantone references are a target, not a guarantee

Brand guides usually specify spot colours such as a Pantone reference. Pantone colours are mixed inks for commercial printing; a wrap printer simulates them in CMYK, and Pantone itself publishes how close each simulation gets. For some references the match is excellent, for others visibly off. Supplying the reference is still essential, because it gives the printer something exact to aim at.

The film, the ink and the profile

Every combination of printer, ink and film has a colour profile, and the result depends on all three. The white point of the film (how white the vinyl itself is) shifts every colour printed on it, and film from different manufacturers is not the same white.

The laminate changes the colour

Printed wraps are laminated, and the laminate is a colour decision. A gloss laminate deepens and saturates; a matte laminate lightens and greys the same print noticeably. Colour has to be matched with the laminate on, not before. Our guide to vehicle wrap laminates explains the options.

Daylight

A proof viewed under office lighting and a van viewed in a parking lot are not the same colour. Outdoor light is the only honest reference for a vehicle.

Printed Film or Solid Colour Vinyl?

This is the biggest decision in wrap colour matching, and it is often made by default. There are two ways to put a brand colour on a vehicle:

  • Print it. The colour is reproduced in ink on white cast film. This is unavoidable for photographs, gradients and complex artwork, and it is fine for most brand colours that sit within the printable range.
  • Cut it from a solid-colour film. 3M's 2080 series and Avery Dennison's Supreme Wrapping Film come in hundreds of factory colours, and lettering and simple shapes can be cut from them. The colour is pigmented through the film, identical on every vehicle you ever wrap, and unaffected by printer profiles or laminate. You can hold the colour chip against the brand guide and decide in daylight. Browse the ranges on our 3M and Avery Dennison colours page and in the manufacturer catalogues.

For a brand whose identity depends on one exact colour, the strongest layout is often a hybrid: a solid-colour film for the main brand colour and lettering, with printed panels only where the artwork needs them. One caution applies even to solid films: 3M notes that film colours vary slightly from lot to lot and advises using one lot for a whole vehicle, so a fleet wrapped over months should be planned with lot numbers in mind.

Metallic, pearl and fluorescent brand colours cannot be printed in standard CMYK at all. If your brand uses one, it is a cut-vinyl colour or it is an approximation.

Wrap Colour Matching You Can Sign Off

  1. Send the right files. Vector logo files, the brand guide with Pantone and CMYK values, and any physical reference such as a printed brochure or a previous vehicle. Our guide to vehicle wrap file types lists what printers need.
  2. Ignore the on-screen proof for colour. It shows layout and placement. It cannot show you the printed colour, because you are looking at it on a screen.
  3. Ask for a printed swatch. A small test print on the actual film, with the actual laminate applied, from the printer that will run the job. Look at it outdoors next to your reference and next to the vehicle's paint.
  4. Agree the tolerance. For colours near the edge of the gamut, the swatch is the colour you will get. Approve it, adjust the brand colour for vehicles, or move that element to cut vinyl.
  5. Print the whole job in one run. All panels for a vehicle, and ideally for a fleet, printed from the same profile, ink set and film lot, then laminated with the same finish.
  6. Keep the approved swatch. It is the reference for any future panel repair or additional vehicle; see our article on vinyl wrap repair for why that matters.

Keeping a Fleet Consistent Over Time

Wrap colour matching on a single vehicle only has to match itself. A fleet has to match the van wrapped last year and the one wrapped next spring. Fading is the enemy: a print that has spent two summers in the sun is lighter than a new one, so a new vehicle parked beside it will look wrong even if the print is identical. Options are to accept the difference, to schedule refreshes so vehicles age together, or to put the main brand colour in solid film, which fades far less visibly than ink. Colour Matching to Paint

Sometimes wrap colour matching is not to a brand guide but to the vehicle: a partial wrap that must blend into the factory paint, or a replacement panel on an existing wrap. Printed film can be colour-matched to a paint sample with a swatch process like the one above, but metallic and pearl paints are impossible to reproduce in print and difficult even in solid film. For those, the design should use a deliberate contrast rather than chase a match that will always be slightly off.
