A wrap is only as good as the surface under it. On a work truck that surface has usually spent a few winters on salted roads, carries a ladder rack and a toolbox, and may still wear the last owner's lettering. Good truck wrap preparation is what turns that truck into something vinyl can bond to for years instead of lifting at the first edge in February. This guide covers what you can do before drop-off, what to leave to the installer, and what 3M and Avery Dennison say a surface must look like before film goes on. It applies to a full wrap, a partial truck wrap and a set of truck decals alike.

Why Truck Wrap Preparation Matters More on a Work Truck

A commuter car arrives at a wrap shop clean, smooth and factory painted. A pickup or box truck that earns its living arrives with bed liner overspray on the tailgate, a rusty chip above the wheel arch, silicone around a light bar and a tonneau cover nobody remembers removing. Each detail changes what can be wrapped, how long it takes and how the result looks.

Both major film manufacturers are blunt about the starting point. Avery Dennison's substrate bulletin says the surface must be original factory paint in good condition, cleaned of all dirt, grease, wax, polish and ceramic or copolymer coatings. 3M's substrate bulletin lists four requirements for any surface: clean, dry, relatively non-porous and smooth. A work truck fails at least one of those more often than not, and the fix is cheaper before the appointment than during it.

Step 1: Decide What Gets Wrapped Before You Prep Anything

Truck wrap preparation depends on coverage. A full truck wrap means every painted panel needs to be wrap-ready, including the ones hidden behind accessories. A partial truck wrap that covers the doors and tailgate leaves the hood and roof alone, so a flaw up there matters less. Truck decals and lettering only need the specific panels they land on to be sound.

Settle the scope first, then prepare only what the design touches. If you are still weighing coverage against budget, our vehicle wrap checklist covers the questions to ask first.

Step 2: Inspect the Paint and Body Honestly

Walk around the truck in daylight and look for four things: rust, chipped or peeling paint, dents and scratches, and any panel that has been repainted.

Rust and bare metal

Vinyl does not seal rust. It hides it for a while and traps moisture against it. Both manufacturers say any painted metal with bare or rust spots should be entirely resurfaced before film is applied, and 3M adds that film should never go directly on carbon steel: corrosion has to be removed and the panel primed or painted first. Our guide to rust repair before a vehicle wrap explains the grades of rust and the repair sequence.

Dents, scratches and chalked paint

Film follows the surface it is applied to, so a dent or deep scratch shows through, more so in gloss. Chalked or oxidised paint has to be buffed off before film can bond, and 3M notes that peeling or bubbling paint means the paint is not attached to the panel, so the wrap will not be either. Read what a wrap does and does not hide before deciding what to repair.

Fresh paint and body repairs

Paint that is dry to the touch is not cured. Solvents keep leaving the coating for days, and film applied too early traps that outgassing as bubbles. The published guidance:

  • 3M says two-part urethane paint must be fully cured before film goes on, that curing generally needs temperatures above 21°C, and that this paint does not cure at all below 10°C.
  • Avery Dennison asks for air-drying paints to sit at 16°C to 25°C in dry conditions for at least one week before film, longer in cold or humid weather, and warns that oil-based enamels can take a month or more.
  • Avery Dennison also states that aftermarket paint is not covered by its film warranty, so a repainted panel is wrapped at the installer's risk. Ask your shop how they handle it.
  • Body filler is porous. 3M recommends sealing it with an epoxy primer or an adhesion promoter before film is applied.

The rule: get bodywork done early, follow the painter's cure schedule, and tell the wrap shop which panels were repainted so they can test adhesion rather than discover it.

Step 3: Deal With Old Graphics and Adhesive First

If the truck already carries lettering, a previous wrap or a dealer sticker, plan to remove it. 3M does allow one graphic over another, but only when the bottom layer is in good condition, well adhered and recommended for that surface, and when the new graphic's edges sit at least 1.3 cm inside or outside the old edges. Old fleet lettering on a sun-baked tailgate rarely meets that standard, and leftover adhesive stops new film from bonding wherever it remains.

Removal takes time on vinyl that has been on for years, and it sometimes reveals paint fade in the shape of the old logo. Our article on how to remove truck decals without damaging paint covers the method. If in doubt, leave it to the shop and have removal quoted as its own line item.

Step 4: Accessories, Racks and Hardware: What to Remove and What to Leave

This is where truck wrap preparation differs most from a car. Every bolt-on part is either removed so film can run underneath it, wrapped around with a cut edge, or left in place where it blocks the design. Decide each one before the appointment:

  • Ladder racks, headache racks and roof racks. If the design runs across the cab roof or bed rails, these usually come off. If only the doors are wrapped, they can stay.
  • Toolboxes and bed covers. A crossover toolbox hides the front of the bed rails. A hard tonneau cover can often be wrapped as its own panel if the material is smooth and painted, but a textured or soft cover cannot.
  • Badges, emblems and nameplates. Film can be trimmed around them or they can be removed and reinstalled. Removal gives a cleaner finish but adds labour, so discuss it.
  • Mud flaps, step bars, trailer hitches and light bars. Anything mounted through a panel leaves a seam where the film has to end. Removing them lets the installer tuck the edge, which is where durability comes from.
  • Aftermarket sealant. Silicone around lights, antennas and rack feet should be cleaned off the painted areas that will be wrapped. 3M lists rubber and caulking materials as surfaces film will not hold on.

If you would rather not remove hardware yourself, ask whether the shop does it and whether reinstallation is included. Either answer is fine, as long as you know before the day.

Step 5: Know Which Surfaces Vinyl Will Not Stick To

Some parts of a truck are not wrap candidates however well they are cleaned, and good truck wrap preparation means knowing them in advance.

  • Spray-in bed liner and textured coatings. 3M's guidance is that adhesive struggles to make contact with any surface rougher than 150-grit sandpaper. A bed liner on a tailgate or rocker panel is far rougher than that.
  • Textured plastic. Unpainted bumper covers, fender flares and mirror caps on work trims are usually textured polypropylene, a low surface energy plastic that both manufacturers flag as difficult. It can sometimes be done after an adhesion test and a primer, but it is never a given; our bumper wrap guide has the detail.
  • Rubber seals and gaskets. Not warranted by either manufacturer. Film ends at the edge of the paint.
  • Rivets and corrugated box panels. These can be wrapped, but they need films and techniques made for them, more time, and a higher minimum application temperature. See our box truck wrap page if that is your vehicle.

A good shop will tell you at the quote stage which parts of your truck fall into these categories and design around them. If a quote promises to wrap a textured bed liner edge to edge, ask how.

Step 6: Wash It, but Skip the Wax

Bring the truck in clean. 3M's vehicle preparation guide says customer vehicles should arrive free of gross contaminants, and that installers should then clean the vehicle again themselves. Your wash is the first pass, not the one the film relies on, so aim to remove mud, salt, grease and bugs rather than to detail the truck.

Do not apply anything after washing. Wax, polish, paint sealant, ceramic spray and tire shine are exactly what the shop then has to strip, and Avery Dennison lists all of them as contaminants that must be gone before application. 3M adds that some automated car wash chemicals can interfere with film adhesion, so a touchless wash a few days ahead is fine, but avoid any wash that advertises a wax or protectant finish in the week before your appointment.

The shop's own truck wrap preparation goes further than anything you can do in a driveway: a wash, compressed air through every seam, solvent for tar and sap, a clay bar pass, and a final wipe with 70 percent isopropyl alcohol and 30 percent water. 3M recommends a dedicated cleaning day before installation so the truck can dry completely and settle at shop temperature, and each panel is cleaned again immediately before film is laid on it.

Step 7: Empty the Cab and the Bed

Take out tools, materials, loose cargo and anything of value. There are practical reasons beyond security:

  • Installers open and close the doors, hood and tailgate dozens of times. Dust from a dirty cab or bed migrates onto panels that have just been cleaned. 3M's guide includes vacuuming the interior for exactly this reason.
  • Door jambs and the inside edges of the tailgate are wrapped or trimmed on a full wrap, so they need to be accessible and clean.

Leave the shop a working key, note any alarm quirks, and make sure the battery is healthy. A truck that will not start on pickup day costs everyone time.

Step 8: Settle the Artwork and Compliance Markings Early

Truck wrap preparation is not only physical. The most common delay on a commercial truck graphics project is artwork that is not ready when the truck is. Before drop-off you should have approved a proof that shows the design on photos of your actual truck, with every logo supplied at print resolution. Our guides on photographing your vehicle for a wrap design and on the file types a printer needs cover what to send.

Commercial vehicles in Ontario may also need regulatory markings, such as a registered name, unit numbers or weight information, depending on the vehicle class and use. These are far easier to design in from the start than to add as separate stickers later. Our fleet decal compliance guide walks through the practical side; confirm your specific obligations with the Ministry of Transportation.

Decide what should be easy to change later, too. Phone numbers, service areas and certifications often go on as separate decals so they can be swapped without redoing a panel.

Step 9: Plan the Downtime and the Season

A work truck off the road is lost revenue, so plan the booking like any other maintenance. Ask the shop for a realistic window for your coverage and condition, then add buffer for removing old graphics or any surface issue that turns up at inspection. If you run several trucks, stage them so the business keeps operating; our article on fleet wrap installation explains how multi-vehicle projects are scheduled.

Temperature matters too. 3M specifies a well-lit, clean, dry indoor space between 16°C and 38°C for vehicle preparation and installation, and films have minimum application temperatures that rise for rivets and complex curves. In the GTA that means a heated bay through the cold months, and a truck brought in from a frosty morning needs time indoors before anyone touches it. Our guide to wrap timing in Toronto looks at the seasons in more detail.

Step 10: Drop-Off Day: What to Bring and What Happens Next

Arrive with the truck washed, emptied and with the agreed accessories removed, and expect to walk around it with the installer. 3M's guide recommends inspecting the vehicle together with the customer for rust, paint issues, scratches and dents before work begins, and a good shop will photograph and note what it finds. That record protects you both: it shows the truck's condition before film and flags any area that needs a repair or a design change before the wrap is cut.

Then the truck moves indoors, is cleaned again, and film goes on panel by panel. Removing old graphics, adhesion tests on repainted or plastic parts, and reinstalling hardware all fit inside the same booking if they were agreed in advance. Surprises found on the day are what stretch a job by days.

Truck Wrap Preparation Checklist

The short version, to work through in the week before your appointment:

  • Coverage decided: full wrap, partial wrap or decals, and which panels each touches.
  • Rust, chips and dents repaired, with paint cured to the painter's schedule and repainted panels disclosed to the shop.
  • Old graphics and adhesive removed, or removal quoted as its own line.
  • Racks, toolboxes, covers, badges and sealant handled: removed, kept, or confirmed as the shop's job.
  • Bed liner, textured plastic, rubber and riveted areas identified and designed around.
  • Truck washed with soap and water; no wax, sealant, ceramic spray or tire shine afterwards.
  • Cab and bed emptied and vacuumed; a working key and battery.
  • Artwork approved on photos of your truck, with compliance markings included.
  • Downtime booked with buffer, in a heated indoor space during the cold months.
  • Time set aside for the joint inspection at drop-off.

After the Install: Give the Film Time

Truck wrap preparation does not end at pickup. Adhesive keeps building its bond over the first days, so follow the aftercare sheet the shop gives you, keep the truck out of automatic washes for the period it specifies, and avoid pressure washing the edges.
